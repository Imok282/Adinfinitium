Research · Nov 2026 · Autonomy

A learned pilot measured against classic guidance.

We flew the same lunar landings twice, once with textbook guidance and once with our pilot, on terrain the pilot had never seen. Here is what changed, and what did not.

What the pilot is

Classic zero-effort-miss / zero-effort-velocity (ZEM/ZEV) guidance always flies the lander. The learned pilot, a 14.8 M-parameter transformer, looks at a 32 × 32 camera crop of the ground, 21 sensor values and the classic command, and adds a small correction to the thrust: at most 30 % of full thrust per axis, clipped, with the engine's tilt and throttle limits still applied. Untrained, it adds zero, so the physics pilot is always the floor.

How we tested it

The test is paired. Every mission — landing cell, starting state, sensor noise and the failures injected into it (engine-out, navigation drift, blind camera) — is generated once from a seed and flown by both pilots. A difference in outcome is then caused by the pilot alone, not by luck of the draw.

  • Maps: three NASA LOLA 5 m/px tiles the pilot never trained on (Site 42, Shoemaker, DM2), 768 missions per map per condition, 2,304 in total.
  • Final exam: Site 23, held out from everything, 1,024 missions per condition, flown once.
  • Conditions: normal failure rates, and harsh (3× the failure rates).
  • Statistics: 95 % confidence intervals on the success rates and their difference, and an exact McNemar test on the missions where exactly one pilot landed.

What we found

At normal failure rates the pilot landed 98.39 % of missions against 97.66 % for classic guidance: +0.74 points, 95 % CI [+0.2, +1.3]. Of the missions where the two disagreed, 32 were landed only by the pilot and 15 only by classic guidance (exact McNemar p = 0.019). Failures fell by about a third.

Under harsh failure rates the pilot was still ahead, 96.92 % against 96.31 % (+0.61 points, CI [−0.2, +1.4], 55 vs 41 discordant missions), but the difference is not significant (p = 0.18).

The whole pooled gain comes from one map. On Site 42, the hardest of the three, the pilot added +1.95 points under normal rates (22 vs 7, p = 0.008). On Shoemaker and DM2 both pilots already land about 99 % of the time; there the two are a tie under both conditions.

The final exam: Site 23

Site 23 was held out from training, from model selection and from every self-improvement round. It was flown once, on 15 November, with the shipped pilot (the same weights as above, SHA-256 unchanged), on 1,024 paired missions per condition. It was not re-run.

On the final exam the pilot is not better than classic guidance. Under normal rates it is an exact tie: each pilot landed 11 missions the other crashed. Under harsh rates the pilot landed 2 missions of 1,024 fewer than classic, a difference that is not significant. Taken with the unseen maps, the fair summary is: about as safe as classic guidance or safer on every map measured, clearly better on Site 42, and a tie on Shoemaker, DM2 and Site 23.

Why the margin is small

  • Classic guidance is already high. It lands 94 to 99 % of missions depending on the map, and the pilot is a residual capped at 0.3 of full thrust, so it can only move the last few percent.
  • The largest failure type is a touchdown slightly too fast sideways. On the validation map Site 11 (2,048 paired missions) the pilot crashed 77 missions; 37 were hard sideways touchdowns, and 27 of those touched down at 0.51 to 0.78 m/s against a 0.5 m/s limit. Classic guidance landed 28 of the 37.
  • A likely cause is that the pilot holds each correction for 0.5 s between decisions while a navigation drift already uses up most of the margin. That is our reading of the touchdown numbers; it was not measured directly.
  • A self-improvement job on 15 November tried four authority rules and three short training rounds to fix this. A gate on Site 11 rejected all seven, so the pilot did not change. Details are in the Dream-RSI post.

Source: records/lander/LANDER_DIAGNOSIS_AND_RECOMMENDATIONS.md section 2; lander_mini/runs/lander/failure_analysis.md.

Limits of this result

This is a simulation, not a flight. The simulator is a simplified 3-DOF model of the last ~2 km of descent, on real NASA LOLA terrain, with failures injected. It treats the lander as a point mass, so attitude dynamics are not modelled, and sensors are noise models, not real hardware.

Hazard labels come from 5 m slope maps, so rocks smaller than 5 m are unknown to both pilots.

The pooled gain under normal rates rests on one map (Site 42). On the held-out final exam, Site 23, the pilot ties classic guidance under normal rates and is not better under harsh rates.

Per-mission outcomes are not saved by the benchmark; the independent check of the intervals and p-values starts from the per-map counts.

What this means

The learned pilot is a correction on top of physics, not a replacement for it. On three unseen maps pooled it lands more missions than classic guidance at normal failure rates, and the gain comes from the hardest map. On the final exam it ties. It never removes the classic controller underneath, and on every map measured it is about as safe as classic guidance or safer.
