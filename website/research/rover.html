Research · Nov 2026 · Rover

Two learned drivers failed. The brain we kept is scripted.

In simulation on real NASA LOLA terrain, a rover leaves the lander, drives to a shadow-edge target and comes back without running its battery flat. Both of our learned drivers failed at this. The brain we present, v3.1, has no learned parameters, and it still loses more than half of its rovers.

The task

The rover drives on 5 m NASA LOLA terrain around the lander. Its target is the edge of a permanently shadowed region: dark and cold, not confirmed ice. It has a 100 Wh battery and draws 2 W while it idles, so a full battery lasts about 50 hours of waiting. Landing hours are random within a month in which the lander is often dark for more than 50 hours in a row. The rover's light forecast is a physics ray-cast with Sun directions from NASA SPICE. Its hazard map comes from our hazard U-Net; in the simulator a hazard costs route cost and score, it cannot lose a rover.

Two scoring rules. Strict: a rover whose battery is empty at any hour, anywhere, is lost; it counts as returned only if it completed the trip without ever being empty. Lenient (the simulator's own rule): an empty battery within 20 m of the lander is forgiven, because the rover sits there and wakes when sunlight returns.

The lenient rule hides most losses, so the headline numbers on this page are strict. A strict number is never compared with a lenient one.

Rover v1 and v2: learned drivers, both failed

Both drivers are the same network, 11,377,354 parameters. v1 was trained with reinforcement learning (PPO) in the overnight run; v2, trained afterwards, learns by copying the physics planners (imitation with DAgger). Both were scored on 8 held-out worlds (the centre windows of Site23 and Site42 on 4 dates, 512 rovers per world) against the simple naive planner, under the simulator's own rule.

Simulation, simulator's own rule. v1 also reached a target in 1.7% of runs against 19.6% for the naive planner. v2 returned 48 of 4,096 rovers; the naive planner returned 380.

  • v1 never returned home in any world. The network that was scored is the one after update 1 of 144: training stopped at the time budget (144 of 800 planned updates).
  • v2 memorised routes. In every training world the lander and the 16 targets sat in the same places. It agrees with the planner on 86% to 93% of return-leg steps on trained terrain and on 15% to 26% on unseen terrain.
  • A self-learning loop in the style of Dream-RSI ran on v2 for 135 minutes. It raised the mean score, but returned stayed at 0.0% in all 38 rounds. It did not learn the mission (more on that loop).

Rover v3: a scripted planner

v3 has no network. For each rover it rolls 36 candidate plans forward inside an exact copy of the simulator (same map, same light forecast, same physics) and picks the best. Three of the 36 are the simple planners themselves, so in an unchanged world v3 cannot lose to them if the code is right. Its lead shows that planning the whole trip with a perfect forecast beats three short-sighted rules. It is not evidence of learning.

On six fresh sites (DM1, LM7, NPA, NPB, NPC, NPD; 24 worlds) v3 returned 59.6% of rovers under the simulator's rule. Counting every flat battery as a loss, it returned 42.0% and lost 56.7%, against 29.8% and 69.4% for the naive planner, the best planner under this rule. It was ahead at 6 of 6 sites. Most of its strict losses were rovers that sat at the lander until the battery went flat: v3 had one place to wait, the lander, and never looked for lit ground.

Rover v3.1: the brain we present

v3.1 is still scripted: 0 learned parameters and no change to the simulator. It adds three things to v3.

  • Stay-alive route search. For each rover it searches a grid of places around it (36 m apart, up to 432 m away) and every remaining hour for the highest battery level it can hold without ever going flat. Read backwards this gives a route: drive to ground that stays lit, wait there, move on when the light moves.
  • 31 new candidate plans next to v3's 36: shelter only, or shelter first and then go to the target and back.
  • Strict-first choice. A plan that returns and is never flat beats a plan that only stays alive, which beats anything else.

Why it still loses more than half

A diagnosis of v3's strict losses, made on development and validation sites before v3.1 was tested, found that the lander is dark for more than 50 hours in a row in 78% of the 300-hour windows, and 98% of the strict losses are such starts. With a 100 Wh battery, 2 W idle draw and a random landing hour, most of these losses probably cannot be planned away in this simulator. How many is a range, not one number: 8% of the losses are proven unavoidable (a loose bound), an energy-only estimate puts the unavoidable share near two thirds, and the upper limit is about 80%. Thermal limits on lit ground are not modelled.

The self-learning layer

v3.1 has a gated self-learning layer. It can propose a corrected map, a corrected solar output, a time shift of the light forecast fitted from the rover's own solar readings, or the measured battery capacity. A proposal is accepted only if it beats the current brain on paired real episodes under the strict rule. It changes settings, not network weights.   • 12 hours late: the rover fitted the +12 h shift at all four sites; the gate accepted it at three. Strict losses there fell from 81.6% to 72.3%, 71.5% to 60.5% and 65.6% to 53.1%.
  • 8 hours early: the shift was measured at all four sites, but no fix was accepted at any of them. A launcher mistake first cut this test short; we completed it afterwards with nothing changed and got the same answer.
  • Hand-written proposal types only. Every change it handled has a proposal type a person wrote (map, solar output, forecast shift, battery capacity). Nothing outside that list was tested. In 36 rounds it accepted 9 changes, 8 of them in round 1 and 1 in round 2: each situation gets at most one useful change, and then the layer stops.
  • Learning costs real episodes, and rovers are lost while it runs. Before any learning, with a late forecast, v3.1 loses more rovers than v3 under the simulator's rule (32.2% against 19.9%): it leaves the lander to shelter on lit ground, and when the forecast is wrong the battery runs out away from the lander.

Method & data

All results are simulation on NASA LOLA 5 m terrain with physics illumination; "target" means a shadow-edge target, not confirmed ice. v3 and v3.1 were evaluated once, under rules written before their test numbers; every v3.1 number was re-derived by an independent checker.
