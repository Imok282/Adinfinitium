Watch it learn.

Training curves from the real logs, including the frozen overnight run that trained four of our networks. Validation is on Site 11, which no model trains on.

Three ways to land

The same 320 simulated descents per policy on NASA LOLA Site 06, with engine-outs, navigation drift and blind cameras injected at raised rates: no guidance landed 0 / 320, classic guidance 314 / 320 and the AI pilot on top of it 315 / 320. This is a visual of the failure modes, not the benchmark; the paired benchmark is in the pilot post.

Source: runs/ghosts_web/lander_lola5m_site06.json (python -m worldmodel.ghosts).

The frozen overnight run

Four of our networks were trained in one unattended run, macmini_night, on a Mac mini (Apple M5 Pro, 18 cores, 64 GB, PyTorch on the Apple GPU), from Saturday 14 November 17:58 to Sunday 15 November 02:01 IST. In 14 stages it downloaded NASA LOLA 5 m terrain, trained the light, hazard, rover and terrain networks, and scored each against its physics backup on two sites it never saw (Site 23 and Site 42).

All 14 stages ended on the first attempt, with no timeout and no retry, using 482.7 of the 717 budgeted minutes. The verification after the run reported 87 checks, 0 failed, 0 warnings. For the rover stage, "ended" means the program finished; it does not mean the rover learned.

Only part of the planned training fitted the time budgets:

The run was then frozen: the SHA-256 checksums of the four checkpoints, the four evaluation files and the code are recorded in runs/FROZEN.json. Nothing was changed after the freeze. Every later experiment, from the rover drivers to the extra checks on fresh sites, is a separate addendum with its own rule written before its held-out numbers; none of them changes a number of this run.

What more training might buy

We fitted a saturating curve to these validation logs and extrapolated it to two and five times the training we actually did. For the hazard, light and terrain networks it projects small gains; these are projections, not measurements, and when the light network really was trained on further, its measured error came out at the weak end of the range projected for twice the training. The projected benchmarks and our roadmap show each one next to the measured value, and the training record lists every run of the two days.

Source: assets/data/projections.json (copy of data/handoff_macmini_1115/projections.json).

The raw log panels

The panels below are the plots written by the frozen run itself, one row per network, by log entry. Flat lines are settings logged with each entry (slope limit, patch size, ray range), not results.

During training the hazard, light and terrain networks improved their validation scores and the rover network did not. On unseen sites the hazard and light networks beat their physics backups, the terrain network lost to a classical kriging fill at predicting hidden laser heights, and the rover network failed. The details are in the hazard model, terrain, rover and benchmarks posts.

What the light model sees and says

The light model predicts the skyline around a cell: how high the terrain rises in every direction. Where the Sun path is above that skyline, the cell is lit. Four cells on held-out Site 23 (an open rim, a typical cell, an enclosed one and the model's worst 5 %), each against an exact ray-cast of the 5 m map and the coarse 20 m ray-cast the model falls back to.

Limits

  • One validation site, Site 11, picked the best hazard, light and terrain checkpoints.
  • The rover's best checkpoint was picked on training rollouts; in this run that rule kept the update-1 network.
  • The Mac mini was shared with other work during the run, so the measured speeds are not those of an idle machine.
  • Everything here is training on real NASA LOLA terrain and evaluation in simulation.
