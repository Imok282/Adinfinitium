Research · Dream-RSI

Self-improvement, behind a gate.

After training stops, our models can keep practising offline. A frozen exam decides whether a new version replaces the old. Most attempts are rolled back; in the lander pilot's latest run, all of them were.

The loop

The planner imagines missions on training terrain and picks the ones its two predictors disagree on most. The pilot finds where it fails (engine-out, navigation drift, blind camera) and practises those cases more often. Both play the cases out in the physics simulators, retrain from the current weights and sit a frozen exam on Site 11. Test sites are never used.

The planner's real log

Five generations sat the exam on the 4090; one dreamt world was skipped because it had no safe cells. One generation was accepted (validation Brier 0.0154 → 0.0135, lower is better); four were rolled back.

The pilot's gate: 0 of 7 accepted

The pilot uses a stricter rule. On 1,024 fixed paired missions on Site 11 (normal and harsh failure rates), a candidate is accepted only if it loses no mission that the current pilot or classic guidance lands, and its total is strictly higher. The current pilot lands 990 of the 1,024; classic guidance lands 965.

On 15 November a job on the Mac mini mined the pilot's failures on the training maps, then sent seven candidates to the gate: four authority rules (how much of the pilot's correction is used, and when classic guidance takes over) and three short training rounds. The gate rejected all seven. The final pilot is the shipped pilot, with the same SHA-256 checksum before and after.

Rule 4 landed one mission more than the current pilot and was still rejected, correctly by the rule: it lost 14 missions that classic or the current pilot lands. The three training rounds had only 4 or 5 updates each within their 12-minute budget, too short to matter, and all three came back worse than the pilot they started from. What worked: the gate never let a worse pilot through. What did not: no improvement was found. The pilot that sat the Site 23 final exam is this unchanged pilot (pilot benchmark).

The rover: a loop in the style of Dream-RSI

Google Research's Dream-RSI has a language model rewrite a small strategy program, then records, replays and scores each round and keeps the best. The original needs paid LLM API keys, so we did not run it. We built a key-free loop in its style for the rover: strategy variants come from a fixed grid of 54 settings written by a person, and a small learned world model is used for practice.

Each round drives 128 rovers for 100 steps, learns a small model from those steps, practises inside it, tests up to 6 candidates on 64 real simulator episodes, and accepts the best only if its gain is above 2 standard errors. It started from rover driver v2, which had already failed its held-out test, and ran by itself for 135 of its 140 minutes.

  • Accepted: 2 of 23 rounds on Site 23 and 3 of 15 on Site 42; the other 33 rounds were rejected.
  • Mean return rose by 8.6 (se 0.8) and 17.4 (se 3.0). On Site 42 the share of rovers lost fell from 90.2 % to 81.6 %.
  • It did not learn the mission. Rovers returned stayed at 0.0 % in all 38 rounds. Reached ice went from 0.0 % to 1.6 % on Site 23 and from 2.3 % to 2.0 % on Site 42.
  • One round on Site 42 (round 5) was accepted on its 64 test episodes while slightly worse on the 256 score starts (−66.2 to −68.1).

The rover brain: a gated self-learning layer

Rover brains v3 and v3.1 are scripted planners, not trained networks. Their self-learning layer changes no weights: it adjusts the belief map, the belief about solar output, an energy margin, charge thresholds and, in v3.1, a time shift of the light forecast and the measured battery capacity. Each proposal must beat the current brain on 128 paired real episodes (gain above 2 standard errors, no fewer returned, no more lost).

  • v3: of 72 rounds, 57 sent a proposal to the gate and 9 were accepted, all in round 1; rounds 2 to 4 accepted 0 of 54. A blocked route improved at 6 of 6 fresh sites, dusty panels at 2 of 6, an unchanged world at none (nothing was accepted). With a light forecast 12 hours late, 0 of 6 sites improved.
  • v3.1: on four sites no rover experiment had touched, 36 rounds in 20 situations sent 23 proposals to the gate; 9 were accepted, 8 of them in round 1.

What works is narrow: a map correction for a blocked route, and a time shift of the light forecast when it is 12 hours late. Every change it can make has a hand-written proposal type; a change without one was never tested. Each situation got one useful change, almost always in the first round, and then the layer stopped. It spends real episodes and loses rovers while it learns.

Limits

  • None of this is open-ended recursive self-improvement. Each loop picks among options a person wrote (rules, settings, proposal types) and keeps a change only if a fixed gate says it is better. None of them changes how it learns.
  • Dreams and lander rounds run only on training sites (01, 04, 06, 07, 20) and the Site 11 gate; Site 23 and the unseen benchmark maps are never used.
  • A single frozen exam site can still be overfitted over many generations; the held-out benchmark is the final check.
  • If nothing passes the gate, nothing changes: the current version keeps flying. For the lander on 15 November, that is what happened.
  • All results are simulation on NASA LOLA terrain. The blocked route and dusty panels are synthetic changes to the simulated world.
