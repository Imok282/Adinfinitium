Research · Terrain

We tested our own headline and it did not survive.

Our 49.9-million-parameter diffusion network looked ten times better than its backup at filling terrain between laser tracks. Against a fair classical competitor on the same hidden laser heights, it lost. Kriging is the terrain estimator we present.

The job

NASA's LOLA 5 m height grid near the lunar south pole is mostly interpolated: only about one pixel in ten holds a real laser return. Anything that reasons about terrain between the tracks needs a way to fill those gaps, and an honest idea of how wrong the fill can be.

We trained terrain_diffusion_mini, a diffusion U-Net with 49,943,681 parameters, in the same frozen overnight run as our other models (7,671 of 8,000 planned steps). Given the laser heights shown to it in a 64 × 64-pixel patch (320 m), it generates six possible versions of the terrain. Their mean is the prediction and their spread is meant to be the uncertainty. Its output is always labelled GENERATED — NOT OBSERVED.

The test

The test is called hide-and-check. In 96 patches per window, half of the real laser pixels are hidden. The method sees the other half and must predict the hidden heights. The error is the RMSE in metres against the hidden real heights. Site 11 was used for choosing settings; Sites 23 and 42 were held out.

In the frozen run the network scored 1.84 m on Site 23 and 2.82 m on Site 42, against 17.92 m and 24.82 m for its backup, a simple Gaussian gap-fill. That was the "ten times better" headline. But the backup was a weak opponent, and NASA reports about 0.37 m for a spline fill of the same product (a different measurement, so not directly comparable). A fair classical competitor was missing.

The fair comparison

After the freeze we wrote a plan, then ran nine classical methods on exactly the same hidden pixels. Each method saw only what the network sees: the shown heights of the patch and where they are. Every setting was chosen on Site 11, and the held-out sites were scored once. The script refuses a second run.

  • Seven of nine classical methods beat the network on both held-out sites, including plain linear interpolation. The network only beats nearest neighbour, inverse-distance weighting and the old backup.
  • Kriging has about half the network's error on Site 23 and 40% of it on Site 42. It is lower in all 13 Site 23 windows and all 6 Site 42 windows.
  • Combining them did not help. A plain average is worse than kriging alone. When the weight was fitted on Site 11, the network got a weight of 0.0064, so the combination is kriging in practice.
  • It held on six fresh sites (DM1, LM7, NPA, NPB, NPC, NPD) that neither model had used: kriging 0.97 m against the network's 1.62 m pooled over 47,074 hidden pixels, and lower on 6 of 6 sites.

The network's uncertainty band failed

A "90%" band should hold the real height about 90% of the time. The network's raw band, the 5–95% spread of its six versions, held it 21.9% of the time on Site 23 and 37.3% on Site 42. The network is far too sure of itself.

We later fitted a single scale factor on Site 11 (8.232) and applied it once to the held-out sites. Coverage rose to 84.9% and 96.9%, which met the criterion we had set in advance of 80–97%. The price is width: the calibrated band is 7.1 m wide on Site 23 and 15.5 m on Site 42, 3.9 and 5.5 times the error. It is honest on average, not sharp, and it is uneven: where the network was most confident, coverage on Site 23 was only 66.7%.

The kriging band covers about as often and is 3 to 5.6 times narrower. It is not exactly a 90% band: it covers 85.9% and 88.2% on the held-out sites, two rough Site 23 windows are below 80%, and it reflects distance from the shown pixels more than roughness. It has no formal guarantee.

Why this test favours interpolation

The hidden pixels lie on the same laser tracks as the shown ones, usually a few pixels away. That is ideal for interpolation. Filling wide gaps with no measurement nearby was never tested, for the network or for kriging, and that is where a generative model might still help.

The network also produces several plausible, textured versions of the terrain, while kriging gives one smooth surface. We did not score that property, so it is not evidence of accuracy.

What we present

  • Kriging of the laser pixels is our terrain estimator: 0.94 m (Site 23), 1.11 m (Site 42) and 0.97 m (six fresh sites) RMSE on hidden laser heights, with a band that covers 85.9%, 88.2% and 92.8%.
  • The diffusion network is an experiment. It is not wired to anything: a search of our hazard, light and rover code found no use of it, and no later stage reads its output.
  • We no longer use the "ten times better than the backup" headline. It was true, but the backup was weak.

Limits

  • One validation site (Site 11) chose every setting. Two chosen settings sit at the edge of their search grid, and the grid was not extended afterwards.
  • The classical methods used only the 64 × 64 patch, like the network. With the whole tile they would probably do better.
  • The network numbers come from one unseeded sampler run; frozen and re-run differ by under 1% pooled, far less than the gap to kriging.
  • On the fresh sites the smoothed thin-plate spline is slightly ahead of kriging (0.960 m against 0.972 m). Kriging had been fixed in advance by the Site 11 rule, so we kept it.
  • The "truth" itself is a laser measurement with its own error. All of this is NASA LOLA terrain of one region and one data product.
