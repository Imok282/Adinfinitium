Research · Hazard model

Seeing slopes from sparse laser returns.

A small U-Net recovers NASA's official slope-hazard map from the roughly one pixel in ten that a laser actually measured. On a site it never saw, it beats the physics rule it backs up. A much larger 60 M variant trained overnight shows where this kind of model breaks: flat ground.

The problem

Near the lunar south pole, NASA's LOLA laser tracks cover the 5 m height grid only sparsely; most pixels are NASA's interpolation. A lander and a rover both need to know which ground is steeper than the 12° limit.

The physics backup has no learned parameters. It hides the interpolated heights, gap-fills the measured ones and marks every pixel with a slope of 12° or more as unsafe. Our model gets the same sparse input and tries to do the same job better.

Our model

Our hazard model is a plain 4-level U-Net with 1,944,337 parameters. Five input channels go in: the gap-filled slope relative to the limit, high-pass height, the laser mask, NASA's error map and the distance to the nearest laser return. One value per pixel comes out: the probability that the pixel is steeper than 12°. The first channel is the backup's own slope, so the network starts from what the backup knows.

It was trained on LOLA 5 m Sites 01, 04, 06, 07 and 20 (checkpoint b0ac044446, 30 epochs). Site 11 picked the checkpoint. Site 23 was used for neither training nor selection.

A 60 M variant from the overnight run

The numbers in this section are for a different, much larger network, not for our model above.

hazard_unet_big is a residual U-Net with multi-head self-attention, 60,361,561 parameters (counted from the checkpoint). It has the same five input channels, four levels with 72 to 576 channels, and 8-head self-attention at the two deepest levels and in the bottleneck. It was trained in one unattended overnight run on 60 tiles from Sites 01, 04, 06, 07, 20, Haworth, Shoemaker and DM2, for 37 of 40 planned epochs. Site 11 picked the checkpoint; Sites 23 and 42 were held out. The run was then frozen with checksums.

Held-out Sites 23 and 42

On the same Site 23 centre window, the 60 M variant scored F1 0.926 against the slope rule's 0.814. Our 1.94 M model scored 0.950 there. The window and the label are the same, as the identical slope-rule score shows. Across more ground, the 60 M variant scored as follows.

Six fresh sites: still ahead, much less accurate

After the freeze, the 60 M variant was scored once on six sites that no model or setting had used: DM1, LM7, NPA, NPB, NPC and NPD, one centre tile each. It was not retrained or tuned.

The variant still beats the slope rule on all six sites, but its accuracy does not carry over. On the four flat sites it misses between 38% and 75% of the unsafe ground. Pooled over the six sites, 70.8% of its alarms are right, against 96.0% and 88.8% on Sites 23 and 42. These sites are flatter and have fewer unsafe pixels and fewer laser returns, so "new place" and "flatter terrain" cannot be separated. Retraining on flat sites was not tested. A different decision threshold and test-time augmentation were checked on Site 11 only and not adopted.

Our 1.94 M model was not tested on these fresh sites. Whether it shares the flat-ground weakness is untested.

Where the variant's map is used

  • In the overnight run's rover simulator, the 60 M variant's probability map sets route cost and a score penalty. A hazard cannot destroy a rover there, so a wrong map changes routes, not losses. On the flat fresh sites, routes were planned with a map that misses about half the steep ground.
  • The lander pilot's camera sees the slope-rule backup's map, not a U-Net's.

Slope is half the story: the same ground under the real Sun

A slope-safe pixel is no use to a lander or rover if it sits in shadow. Drag the slider to step through September 2027 at Site 23: the Sun never climbs more than about 5° above the horizon, so shadows sweep across the whole massif.

Limits

  • Most of the truth is interpolation. The label for both models is NASA's official 5 m slope map, and about 90% of it rests on NASA's interpolation between laser tracks. A score on all pixels mostly measures agreement with that interpolation.
  • The comparison leans towards the networks. The backup's slope and NASA's error map are among the inputs of both models.
  • Narrow tests. Our model was scored on one held-out window. The 60 M variant's training is dominated by one crater: Haworth provides 19 of its 60 training tiles (32%). One validation site, Site 11, picked both checkpoints.
  • Slope only. The label is a 12° slope rule at 5 m. It does not include rocks, roughness or the lander's footprint. A safe-looking pixel is not proof that ground is safe; shadowed and unmeasured ground stays unknown.
  • All results are on NASA LOLA terrain of one region and one data product. This is model output, not a flight result.
